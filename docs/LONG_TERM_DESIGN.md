# Long-Term Design

Everything this game will eventually contain, designed now and sequenced in
`ROADMAP.md`. Nothing here is built yet. Nothing here is forbidden — it is
ordered.

This document exists so the whole game is designed on paper, and so any future
contributor (human or AI) can see where the project is heading without
re-deriving it.

---

## 1. Design principles that must survive every layer

These are the constraints that later features are allowed to respect.

1. **Observation is the primary verb.** The player watches, understands, and
   occasionally intervenes. Adding a feature that is only interesting to *operate
   manually* is a mistake.
2. **Every event must be explainable.** The report states what happened and why,
   from recorded data. No post-hoc storytelling.
3. **Consequences must be persistent.** If nothing can be lost, nothing matters.
4. **Autonomy is the default.** Personnel act without orders. The player's tools
   are policy and exception, not direct control.
5. **Complexity arrives through interaction, not count.** Five connected systems
   beat twenty disconnected ones.

---

## 2. The needs ladder

Needs are added one at a time, in this order, because each new need multiplies
the number of interactions that must be tuned.

| Stage | Needs | Effect on play |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hunger, fatigue | Daily cycle. Predictable. |
| 2 | + Hydration | Faster-burning resource. Water is a supply line, not a number. |
| 3 | + Morale | A slow-moving proxy for how the base is being run. |
| 4 | + Injuries | Individual, persistent, occasionally permanent. |

**Morale** is deliberately last among the soft needs. Morale is the easiest
need to add badly, because it tends to become a mood-ring that only reflects
whatever the player just did. It should instead be computed from underlying
observable state — food security, rest, workload, injuries, recent events — so
it is always explainable.

**Injuries** arrive with two important properties:

- They are **individual and persistent**. A named person is hurt for days.
- They are **the consequence of a choice**, not random punishment. Tired people
  who work anyway get injured. The player is offered the choice (D2) and can
  accept the risk knowingly. That makes injury dramatic rather than unfair.

---

## 3. Injury and attrition

- Injuries have severity, cause, and a recovery time.
- Recovery time depends on treatment quality — which depends on staffing and
  supplies. A base with no medic recovers slowly.
- Severe injuries may leave a lasting limitation (reduced work rate, cannot do
  one class of job). Not necessarily permanent death at this stage.
- **This creates the game's best decision moments**: work someone who's hurt,
  accept the risk, or accept the slowdown.

## 4. The production layer (factory / engines)

This is the layer that turns the game from "people simulator" into something
closer to a factory game with a soul. It is planned, not built.

**Design intent:** the base becomes a small production system whose bottlenecks
are *people*, not machines. That is the game's identity — a factory game where
the factory workers are characters.

### 4.1 Power and engines

- Engines (generators) consume fuel and wear out.
- Unplanned downtime forces decisions about who works where and when.
- Power is a good first production system because its failure modes are
  legible and its resource (fuel) is easily understood.

### 4.2 Workshop and manufacturing

- A maintenance workshop that consumes raw materials.
- Repair vs. manufacture as a genuine tradeoff: do you fix the generator you
  have, or build a spare you don't need yet?
- Equipment condition is persistent and degrades with use.

### 4.3 Supply chain

- Deliveries arrive on a schedule that is *imperfect* — late, partial,
  occasionally wrong. Generated by rules, not scripted.
- Storage capacity forces decisions about what to keep.
- A supply shortage is the most reliable source of the "small anomaly" the
  player notices on an otherwise normal day.

### 4.4 What the factory layer must NOT become

No conveyor-belt optimisation puzzle. The bottleneck should always be human
decisions, not throughput maths. If a player can lose by building a more
efficient layout, the game has wandered into a different genre.

---

## 5. Vehicles

Planned after the production layer works.

- Vehicles are a **big** commitment: they consume fuel, need maintenance, need
  a driver, and go where you send them.
- Vehicle condition is the first place the equipment-condition system earns its
  keep at scale.
- Vehicles are the natural bridge toward the external layer (see §7), because a
  vehicle can leave the base and not come back.

---

## 6. Relationships, secrets, achievements

Requested directly, and correct — this is how a sim stays interesting after the
systems are known.

### 6.1 Relationships

- People who repeatedly work together accumulate a small opinion of each
  other.
- Deliberately **simple**: a value per pair, plus a threshold that surfaces a
  line of text in the report. No branching dialogue trees.
- Relationships are a *reporting* layer first: they make the event log read as
  human rather than mechanical. A sim where "Amina and Bakary argued" is more
  memorable than "Amina and Bakary both contended for job #4."

### 6.2 Continuity after loss

Supports the D17 position that a loss can still be worth something:

- **Persistent history.** A run leaves a record: who served how long, what
  happened, how it ended.
- **Secrets** — rare, rule-generated anomalies with low probability and high
  narrative weight.
- **Achievements** — long-horizon milestones that persist across runs.

These are what make the game replayable and give a failed base value beyond the
failure.

---

## 7. The external layer (parked)

Raids, enemy AI, operations, faction warfare. Designed at the level of *why it
exists* so it can be added without betraying the core:

- The purpose is to make the base's accumulated state matter. A raid is only
  interesting if there is something to lose.
- It is last because it requires a full economy, equipment, and people to be
  worth attacking.
- **Recommendation:** do not start here. If it ever ships, it must be additive
  and optional, so the peaceful base remains a valid way to play.

---

## 8. What the game is not

Restating the exclusions so they cannot be quietly re-added:

- Not a clicker. The player should not be able to solve everything with one
  button.
- Not a micromanager. Direct control of individual actions is not a goal.
- Not an optimisation puzzle. Systems should be understandable, not solvable by
  a spreadsheet.
- Not a combat game. Combat is optional garnish, not the point.
- Not infinite. A base has a natural size; growth should be slow and
  meaningful.

---

## 9. Known design risks in this long-term plan

Recorded honestly so they are not rediscovered painfully:

1. **Too many needs dilutes each one.** Five needs means five sets of tuning and
   a player who cannot tell which one matters.
2. **Morale is easy to implement badly.** See §2.
3. **The factory layer can swallow the game's soul.** A base that reads as
   production chains with named variables has lost. This is the single biggest
   risk in this document.
4. **Achievements and secrets can feel like filler** if added before the base
   simulation is genuinely interesting.
5. **Vehicle + external layer together** is a large project. Each is a game in
   its own right if built badly.
