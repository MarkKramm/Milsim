## Part 1 — The big three

These three decide whether the game exists. Please think about these hardest.

---

### D1. Does the base have something to lose?

*Why this matters: a simulation where nothing can be lost is a screensaver.
This is the most important decision in the document.*

- **A. No stakes.** Hunger and fatigue exist, nothing permanent is lost.
  Nobody ever quits, gets hurt, or leaves.
- **B. Soft failure spine [R]** *(medium confidence)* — one "unit readiness"
  number that drifts down when people are hungry, tired, or idle. If it hits
  zero, people **quit** and you have a staffing crisis.
- **C. Hard fail.** Lose the game if readiness hits zero. Restart.
- **D. Injuries and permanent consequences.** People get hurt, equipment
  breaks for good. Most dramatic, most work.

**My pick: B.** Cheapest option that creates real stakes, and it is roughly
30 lines of code. I deliberately avoided C and D because they add pressure but
also add frustration, and you have never yet played this game.

### D2. What does the player actually decide?

*Why this matters: right now the player's only actions are sliders and
dropdowns. Sliders are the weakest form of agency in a simulation game
because you cannot predict the effect, so you never feel responsible.*

- **A. Sliders only** — set priorities, allocate resources, inspect.
- **B. Named-option decisions [R]** *(medium confidence)* — a few times a
  day the sim surfaces a concrete situation with 2–3 named options. e.g.
  *"Amina is too tired to finish the repair. (a) push through and risk injury,
  (b) hand off to Bakary and lose the slot, (c) leave it broken."* They wait
  in the report; they do not interrupt.
- **C. Free-form orders** — type anything you want a soldier to do.
  Maximum agency, requires real AI, will not work at this stage.
- **D. Full interruption** — every decision halts the sim instantly.

**My pick: B.** It is the difference between a sim and a game, and it does not
conflict with your "do not interrupt me" preference because these *queue up*
and you decide in one sitting.

### D3. What makes a normal, uneventful day interesting?

*Why this matters: a normal day is 95% of what you will ever see. If those
are boring, the game is boring.*

- **A. Small anomalies [R]** *(medium confidence)* — the base occasionally
  has something slightly off: a tool runs hot, a delivery is late, two
  people need the same thing. Generated by rules, never scripted.
- **B. Relationships** — people who work together form opinions of each other.
  Great character, risks needing real AI.
- **C. Player-set goals** — you write a loose objective ("keep everyone fed
  through the shortage") and the report tracks it. Cheapest of all.
- **D. Nothing.** A calm day is a calm day. Sometimes that is enough.

**My pick: A, with a seed of C.** A because it is the most "simulation" answer
and the least risky; C because making the player a participant rather than a
spectator is nearly free.

### D4. How fast does a day pass?

*Concrete numbers matter more than the abstract debate in `CORE_LOOP.md`.*

- **A. ~12 s/day** — too fast, you never form attachment to anything.
- **B. ~3 min/day [R]** *(medium confidence)* — 1 tick = 1 in-game minute,
  8 ticks/sec at 1×, day = 1440 ticks = 3 minutes.
- **C. ~8 min/day** — slow and atmospheric. Risk: you alt-tab.
- **D. Speeds only, no default** — pause, 1×, 4×, 16×, and you choose.

**My pick: B,** with 16× added as a "skip to report" button (11 s/day).
This is cheap to test and easy to change — a one-line constant. I am *unsure*
and I think you should just play it and tell me which feels right.

### D6. How do you avoid agents dithering?

*Two workers flip-flopping between tasks every tick looks broken even when
nothing is wrong.*

- **A. Hysteresis [R]** *(high confidence)* — a worker must finish or
  explicitly abandon a task before picking a new one, and abandoning costs a
  little progress.
- **B. Commit fully** — once a task is picked, it always completes. Simple,
  but makes people ignore emergencies.
- **C. Nothing.** Let it dither and see.

**My pick: A.** This belongs in the spec from day one; retrofitting it later
means editing every job rule.

**Your answer:**
sure up to you R

### D7. What is the pressure — time or people?

- **A. Time pressure [R]** *(medium confidence)* — shortages, deliveries that
  are late, equipment that degrades. The base slowly drifts toward needing
  your attention.
- **B. People pressure** — personality clashes, requests for leave, someone
  quitting.
- **C. External pressure** — outside events push in. This is where raids and
  war come in, and I recommend **against** it for the prototype.
- **D. None.** Peaceful sandbox.

**My pick: A,** drifting toward B as the soft-failure spine from D1 develops.
Explicitly *not* C yet — that is the most expensive possible way to add
pressure, and it is the thing most likely to kill this project.

### D9. The architecture rule

- **A. Simulation is a pure module [R]** *(high confidence)* — no React
  imports, takes state, returns new state, writes events. Must be runnable in
  a terminal with no browser.
- **B. Mixed** — convenient early, painful later.

**My pick: A,** without exception. This one rule is what lets the game survive
to Stage 6 and beyond. If a React component ever contains a rule about how the
world works, that is a bug.
