# Audit & Recommendations — Military Base Simulator

Status: **draft for review**. Nothing here is implemented. Nothing here is locked.
This document audits the original 7 planning docs, then proposes improvements.

Read this if you want to know *why* something is suggested.
To actually make decisions, read `DECISIONS_TO_APPROVE.md` instead.

---

## 0. Verdict on the original plan

The ChatGPT planning pack is genuinely good. It is honest about what is
undecided, it refuses to lock architecture around imagined future features,
and it correctly identifies one testable question for the prototype.

Do **not** send it back for a rewrite. Keep it as the vision document.

Where it is weak is not quality — it is **coverage**. It describes a
simulation but never describes a *game*. Read section 1; this is the single
most important thing in this audit.

---

## 1. The biggest problem: there is no game, only a screensaver

What is described is: agents do jobs, needs go up and down, resources move,
a report explains it. That is a **simulation**. Sims are not automatically
fun games. Dwarf Fortress is a spreadsheet that people enjoy. Theme Park is
an accounting program that people enjoy. The difference is that both have
**stakes, goals, and failure**.

The current plan has, deliberately, none of the three:

- **No stakes.** Nothing bad can happen permanently. If a soldier starves,
  what is lost? The plan does not say. If nothing is lost, hunger is a
  number that wiggles.
- **No goal.** The player has no reason to want Tuesday instead of Monday.
  "Keep watching" is not a goal.
- **No failure.** No way to lose. A game where you cannot lose is a
  screensaver you are permitted to press stop on.

This is not a reason to abandon the plan. It is a reason to add **one** of
each, cheaply, before Stage 3.

### Recommendation — add a soft-failure spine

Pick one resource that, if it hits zero for too long, permanently damages
something. For example:

- **Morale / readiness of the unit** as a single number. It drifts down when
  people are hungry, tired, or idle, and up when they are fed, rested, and
  working. If it hits 0, people **quit**. You now have a staffing crisis.

This is extremely cheap to implement (one number, one drift rule, one
consequence) and it converts the whole simulation from "watching" into
"tending." It also answers `OPEN_QUESTIONS.md`'s question *"What creates
pressure without making the player feel constantly punished?"* — answer:
slow drift, not sudden spikes.

Then add **one lightweight objective**, not a campaign. For example, a
standing order that arrives each in-game week: *"Maintain the base."*
Graded pass/fail, no win screen. This gives Tuesday a reason to exist.

**Do not add combat, raids, or a war layer to solve this.** Those are the
most expensive possible fix and they are listed as out of scope. The soft
failure spine above costs perhaps 30 lines of code.

## 2. The "boring day" problem

`OPEN_QUESTIONS.md` correctly asks: *"What makes a normal day interesting
when nothing dramatic happens?"* and then never answers it. This is the
question that decides whether the game works, because a normal day is what
the player will see 95% of the time.

Three cheap answers, pick one, do not build all three:

1. **Small visible anomalies.** The base occasionally has something slightly
   off — a tool runs hot, a delivery is late, two people both need the same
   thing. Nothing catastrophic, but legible. This is the most "simulation"
   answer and the least risky.
2. **Personality / relationships.** People who work together repeatedly
   develop a small opinion of each other. Cheap, adds enormous character.
   Risky: drifts toward needing real AI.
3. **Player-introduced goals.** The player writes down their own objective
   ("keep everyone fed through the shortage") and the game just reports on
   it. Cheapest by far, and it makes the player a participant instead of a
   spectator.

### Recommendation

**Option 1 for the prototype, with a seed of option 3.** Anomalies are
generated by the rules (not scripted stories — the original plan is right
about that), and the player can set one loose goal the report tracks.

## 3. Player agency is currently too abstract

The plan's stated player actions are: *"set work priorities, allocate
resources, inspect."* Those are sliders and dropdowns. Sliders are the
weakest possible form of player agency in a simulation game, because the
player cannot predict the consequence and therefore does not feel responsible
for the outcome.

The plan also says the player should not be interrupted. Both of those
sentences together mean **the player has almost nothing to do**. That is a
real design risk, and it is not fixed by adding more sliders.

### Recommendation

Keep the low-interrupt philosophy, but guarantee that at least a few times
per day the simulation surfaces a **concrete situation with two or three
named options**, and the player picks one. Not a dialog box interrupting a
cutscene — a small panel in the report: *"Amina is too tired to finish the
generator repair. She can (a) push through, risking injury, (b) hand off to
Bakary and lose the repair slot, (c) you do nothing and it stays broken."*

This is exactly what `OPEN_QUESTIONS.md` calls a "consequential decision," and
it also solves the interruption worry: it does not interrupt, it *waits in the
report* and applies its consequence at the next decision point. The player
can batch three of them and decide in one sitting.

This is the single highest-leverage change in this document. A sim where the
player picks between named tradeoffs is a game. A sim where the player
adjusts sliders is not.

## 4. Time model — the original plan's biggest open risk

The plan compares continuous ticks, daily turns, and a hybrid, then
recommends ticks + pause/speed without picking a tick length or a day length.
That is the actual decision, and it is missing.

You cannot evaluate "is watching fun" without a number for how long a day
takes. If a day is 12 seconds, the player never forms attachment. If a day is
8 minutes, they will alt-tab.

### Recommendation

Make these concrete numbers, and put them in the spec:

- 1 tick = 1 in-game **minute**.
- 1 in-game day = 1440 ticks.
- At 1× speed, run **8 ticks per second** → an in-game day = **3 minutes**.
- Speeds: pause, 1×, 4×, 16×. At 16× a day is 11 seconds, which is a usable
  "skip to the report" button.
- Needs change on a timescale of hours, not minutes, so at 1× the player
  sees meaningful change every ~20 seconds without anything flickering.

Then **test 1× vs 16× honestly**: play three days at each and ask which one
you wanted to keep playing. That is a cheap, decisive experiment and it
answers the biggest open question in the pack.

Also recommended: **do not display a live clock counting seconds.** Show
"Day 3 — Afternoon." Abstract time reads better than a fast-ticking clock
and avoids the player watching a number they cannot influence.

## 5. Stack recommendation

The plan deliberately left this open. That was correct at planning time; it
now needs closing so Stage 1 is not blocked.

**Recommendation: TypeScript + Vite + React, simulation as a pure module.**

Why:

- You already have a working Vite + TypeScript + Tailwind project
  (`~/special-day-build`). Zero new tooling to learn, and the muscle memory
  transfers directly. This is the strongest argument and it is a practical
  one, not an aesthetic one.
- Vite runs fine in Termux. You can serve the game locally and open it in
  your phone browser, or run it on a desktop from the same repo.
- **React earns its place here** because this game's UI is *many small
  panels that all update every tick* (roster, job board, resource counts,
  event log). That is exactly the case React is good at. A vanilla-DOM
  version will have you hand-writing update code for every panel for no
  learning benefit.
- **Tailwind** for layout only, not for anything clever.

The important architectural rule, which the original plan already states and
which I want to keep loud:

> The simulation must be a plain TypeScript module with **no React imports**.
> It takes a state, returns a new state, and writes to an event log. The UI
> reads state and calls commands. If a React component ever contains a rule
> about how the world works, that is a bug.

That single rule is what will let this game survive to Stage 6. Keep the
simulation a thing you could run in a terminal with no browser at all.

## 6. Things the plan should add (cheap, high value)

These are not scope creep. Each is small and each prevents a class of pain
later.

### 6.1 Seeded randomness from tick zero
The plan mentions seeded randomness as optional. Make it **mandatory from the
first line of code**: `state.seed`, and every random draw goes through one
function. You cannot add it later without retro-fitting every system. The
payoff is enormous — you can reproduce any bad run exactly, which is the
difference between debugging in minutes and debugging in hours.

### 6.2 An event log that is the single source of truth for reports
The plan says reports should summarize real events rather than invent causes.
Make it structural: **reports read the event log; they never recompute
anything.** Every meaningful state change appends an event with enough context
to explain itself later. This makes the "report is accurate" acceptance
criterion true by construction instead of by discipline.

### 6.3 A debug view
A panel showing the last 50 events with raw data, plus a "copy run as JSON"
button. Ten minutes of work. When something looks wrong at 16× speed, you
will want it constantly.

### 6.4 Task-switch hysteresis
`OPEN_QUESTIONS.md` asks *"How do we prevent workers repeatedly switching
tasks?"* and leaves it open. The standard fix is simple and should be in the
spec from the start: **a worker must finish or abandon a task before picking
a new one, and abandoning has a cost.** Set the cost slightly above "a better
task just appeared." Without this you get dithering, and dithering looks
broken even when nothing is wrong.

### 6.5 Deterministic tie-breaking
When two workers both want the same job, resolve by a stable rule (lowest ID
first), never randomly. Random tie-breaks make behaviour unreproducible and
are the classic source of "it only happens sometimes" bugs.

## 7. Things the plan correctly excludes — keep excluding them

Agreeing with the plan on these, so it is on the record:

- No combat, raids, enemy AI, or war layer in the prototype. These are the
  features most likely to kill this project before it is fun, because they
  are the most fun-sounding and the most expensive.
- No pathfinding. Abstract "worker spends 20 minutes at the workshop" is
  enough and takes minutes to build.
- No morale/hydration/injuries/sleep-quality as separate needs. Two needs
  (hunger, fatigue) is the correct number for a prototype.
- No 3D, no animation, no sprite work.
- No save/load until the state model stops changing.
- No weapon database or equipment catalog.

Also worth adding to the exclusion list: **no hiring/recruitment tree, no
skill trees, no progression UI.** Those feel mandatory because they are in
every other game, but they are pure scope creep at this stage.

## 8. Risk register

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Watching is not fun without pressure | **High** | Fatal | Section 1 soft-failure spine — do not defer past Stage 3 |
| Player has nothing meaningful to do | **High** | Fatal | Section 3 named-option decisions |
| 1× / 16× speed both wrong | Medium | High | Section 4 concrete numbers, test early |
| Scope grows past the prototype | High | High | Section 7 exclusions; treat new ideas as parking lot |
| Simulation logic leaks into UI | Medium | High | Section 5 rule; keep sim importable in a terminal |
| Dithering / stuck loops | Medium | Medium | Section 6.4 hysteresis |
| Phone screen too small for many panels | Medium | Low | Design mobile-first, one panel per screen |
| Burnout before the fun evaluation | Medium | High | Keep Stage 1–2 to a few evenings; ship ugly early |

The two High/Fatal rows are the ones worth acting on. Everything else is
manageable.

## 9. What I would change in the original documents

Small edits, if you agree:

- `CORE_LOOP.md` — add a step between "Resolve interactions" and "Explain":
  **"Decide"**, where the player resolves one pending situation.
- `GAME_VISION.md` — add a third pillar: **"Consequences that last."**
  Currently "persistent" is claimed but nothing in the design guarantees
  anything is actually lost.
- `PROTOTYPE_SPEC.md` — replace acceptance criterion 3 ("at least two systems
  interact") with a stronger one: at least one system interaction must produce
  a consequence the player can point at and regret.
- `OPEN_QUESTIONS.md` — delete "What makes a normal day interesting" from
  *unresolved* once Section 2 is decided.

## 10. The one thing to keep in mind

You will be tempted, repeatedly, to skip ahead — to the vehicle system, the
raids, the weapon condition model, because they are the exciting parts. The
original plan's instinct is right and I want to reinforce it:

> The exciting parts are why you started. The boring part — a base where
> nothing happens and you can explain why — is the part that makes it a game.

Ship the boring version. If the boring version is fun, everything after it is
easy. If it is not fun, no amount of raids will save it.
